\title{Variable importance is variance, not effect size:\\
a caution and a prevalence screen}
\author{Christian Theil Have}
\date{July 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Mean-decrease-in-impurity (MDI) variable importance is the default, cheapest way
to read a random forest regression, and it is routinely misread as a per-unit effect size,
as if it were comparable to a linear-model coefficient. In additive settings MDI
instead estimates each feature's \emph{variance contribution},
$\mathrm{Var}(f_j(X_j))$ --- for a linear term, $\beta_j^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X_j)$. We
make the practical consequences concrete through five controlled experiments:
impurity importance (i) grows with the \emph{square} of a linear coefficient, not
the coefficient; (ii) assigns an interaction's variance to the interacting
variables; and (iii) gives \emph{provably identical} scores to a genuinely non-linear
effect and to a linear effect on a skewed feature, because one is a monotone
relabelling of the other and a tree sees only feature order. These are not artefacts of the well-known bias toward
high-cardinality, continuous, or correlated features: within each experiment the
features are uncorrelated and all of one kind (all continuous, or all binary),
so that bias applies equally to all of them, and where their distributions differ
that difference is the object of study rather than a confound. Nor are they
specific to impurity: permutation importance behaves the same way, and
standardised regression coefficients spread similarly. No marginal importance
measure separates an effect's \emph{shape} from a feature's \emph{distribution};
partial-dependence plots do. Finally, an LLM-powered screen of the ranger-citing
literature suggests that about one
in six papers that interpret an impurity ranking rely on it without such
corroboration, including heavily-cited work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background-strands}

For a random forest~\cite{breiman2001}, the impurity importance of a feature is
the total decrease in node impurity attributable to splits on that feature,
summed over all splits and all trees. For regression, \code{ranger}~\cite{wright2017}
(and most implementations) score a split by how much it reduces the sum of
within-child \textbf{response variances}. For classification the impurity is the
Gini index, which measures the same thing:
$1 - \sum_k p_k^2 = \sum_k \mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{1}\{Y = k\})$, the total variance
of the class indicators. Impurity importance is a variance-explained quantity in
both settings, and that is the source of every effect studied here.

Two strands of prior work bear on this. The first documents that MDI is
\emph{biased} toward continuous and high-cardinality features and inflates
correlated predictors~\cite{strobl2007,nembrini2018}; Nembrini et al.\ give the
bias-corrected ``actual impurity reduction'' (AIR), exposed by \code{ranger} as
\code{impurity\_corrected}. The second is theoretical: Louppe et
al.~\cite{louppe2013} decompose asymptotic MDI into per-variable contributions,
and Scornet~\cite{scornet2023} proves that for additive regression with
independent inputs MDI recovers a \emph{variance-explained} decomposition, each
feature's score tracking $\mathrm{Var}(f_j(X_j))$, which for a linear term is
$\beta_j^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X_j)$.

Our experiments are consequences of that identity, not new biases. Their value is
to show, among uncorrelated features that are all of one kind --- all continuous,
or all binary, so that a bias between feature types cannot separate them --- how
far a variance-contribution measure departs from a per-unit effect size, and that it
\emph{provably cannot} separate an effect's shape from a feature's distribution.
The new contribution is thus the sharp skew-versus-nonlinearity demonstration and
an estimate (Sections~\ref{sec:prevalence-method}--\ref{sec:prevalence-results})
of how often the misreading reaches published conclusions.

All experiments use \code{ranger} 0.18.0; the code that generates them and the
figures is available with the rest of the supporting material
(Section~\ref{sec:availability}). Unless noted, importances are the plain
\code{impurity} measure (the one the definition above describes);
Section~\ref{sec:linear} compares it against \code{impurity\_corrected}.

\section{Linear effects: importance is in squared-coefficient units}
\label{sec:linear}

Data: $y = a + 2b + 4c$, with $a,b,c \sim U(0,1)$, $n = 1000$. OOB
$R^2 = 0.98$. Impurity importance (abbreviated ``vip'', variable importance)
ranks the features in the correct order, but its \emph{ratios} do not match the
coefficient ratios:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rccc}
\toprule
feature & OLS coef & impurity vip (ratio) & impurity\_corrected (ratio) \\
\midrule
a & 1 & 1.00 & 1.00 \\
b & 2 & 2.39 & 6.23 \\
c & 4 & 7.76 & 25.9 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A coefficient ratio of $1:2:4$ becomes an importance ratio of $1:2.4:7.8$: the
ordering is correct and the growth is clearly super-linear, but the spread 
is not exactly the $1:4:16$, the ratio of the \emph{squared} coefficients that
Section~\ref{sec:background-strands} predicts. 

For a linear term the contributed
variance is $\beta_j^2\,\mathrm{Var}(X_j)$, and here $\mathrm{Var}(X_j)=1/12$ is
common to all features, so importance should track $\beta_j^2$. 

The difference to this expectation is due to the number of features drawn as
candidates at each node (a configurable parameter called \code{mtry}), intended
to decorrelate the trees. In \code{ranger} it defaults
to $\lfloor p/3 \rfloor$, here $1$: each node must split on the
single feature it drew, so $a$ takes splits it would never win in competition and
is inflated at the expense of $c$. Letting features compete removes the dilution
(means over seeds $1$--$10$, $n=1000$, 500 trees):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{clc}
\toprule
\code{mtry} & impurity vip (ratio) & \\
\midrule
1 (default at $p=3$) & $1:2.25:7.26$ & compressed \\
2                    & $1:3.93:15.5$ & $\approx$ predicted $1:4:16$ \\
3 ($=p$, no split randomisation) & $1:4.45:19.1$ & overshoots \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At $\code{mtry}=p$ the
strongest feature wins the root split in every tree, and overshoots. The gap at
the default is just a property of the split randomisation.

Extending to
$y = a + 2b + 3c + \dots + 11k$ (11 features, coefficients $1\ldots11$) demonstrates
this. Over seeds $1$--$10$, regressing importance on $\beta^2$ gives a mean
$R^2$ of $0.98$ (range $0.96$--$0.99$) against $0.91$ on $\beta$ (range
$0.88$--$0.94$), and $\beta^2$ is the better fit on every seed;
Fig.~\ref{fig:superlinear} shows one of them. Importance is denominated in
squared-coefficient (variance) units rather than coefficient units.

The bias correction behaves differently. \code{impurity\_corrected} (AIR) is also
variance-based, so it is no closer to being an effect size --- but it is nearly
\code{mtry}-insensitive: over seeds $1$--$10$ its ratios are $1:4.4:17.5$ at the
default, $1:4.1:16.6$ at $\code{mtry}=2$, and $1:4.1:16.8$ at $\code{mtry}=3$,
near the predicted $1:4:16$ throughout. It is AIR, not plain \code{impurity},
that recovers the variance identity without a tuning caveat. The price is
sampling variance: over the same ten seeds AIR's $c$ ratio spans $10.2$--$25.9$,
against $6.3$--$8.1$ for plain impurity, so the single draw in the table above is
one of its extremes and no single-seed AIR ratio should be read closely.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig1_superlinear.png}
\caption{Impurity importance against the coefficient $\beta$ (left, curved) and
against the variance contribution $\beta^2$ (right, linear, $R^2=0.99$).
Importance is linear in $\beta^2$, not in $\beta$.}
\label{fig:superlinear}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Takeaway.} Among identically distributed features with linear
effects, importance ranks correctly, though ``how much more'' is not comparable
to a coefficient. An interaction lets a variable with no
marginal effect outrank one that has (Section~\ref{sec:interactions}), and
unequal feature distributions reorder the ranking on their own
(Section~\ref{sec:confound}).

\section{Interactions are charged to the interacting variables}
\label{sec:interactions}

Data: a pure three-way parity interaction with no marginal or pairwise effects,
\[
  y = d/10 + \big((a + b + c) \bmod 2\big), \qquad a,b,c,d \in \{0,1\}, \quad n = 10000.
\]
No single variable, and no pair, among $a,b,c$ carries information about $y$;
only the joint parity of all three does. $d$ has a small independent effect.
(Two-way parity would not do: for binary inputs $(a+b)\bmod 2 = a+b-2ab$ is
bilinear, so OLS with an $a{\times}b$ term fits it exactly. Three-way parity is
the smallest a standard interaction term cannot recover.)

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{OLS} stays blind even with all pairwise products: a model in
  $a,b,c$ and $a{\times}b, a{\times}c, b{\times}c$ still reaches only
  $R^2 = 0.01$, all coefficients ${\approx}0$ except $d$ (${\approx}0.10$). Only
  the three-way product $a{\times}b{\times}c$ recovers the signal, which one
  rarely adds unprompted.
\item \textbf{Random forest} captures it (OOB $R^2 = 0.73$), and impurity
  importance loads on the interacting variables. Averaged over seeds $1$--$10$,
  $a$, $b$ and $c$ score $398$ (SD $51$) against $23$ for $d$
  (Fig.~\ref{fig:interaction} shows one seed): the three variables with no
  marginal effect outrank the only variable that has one by a factor of
  ${\sim}17$, on every seed.
\end{itemize}

Since $a$, $b$ and $c$ are exchangeable by construction, any difference among
their importances is sampling noise, and within a single seed they spread by
$7$--$72\%$ (median $28\%$): only the $a,b,c \gg d$ gap carries information here.
That figure belongs to this design, which is a noisy one --- the signal becomes
available only once three nested splits line up, and OOB $R^2$ is $0.73$.
Replacing the parity with equal \emph{marginal} effects on the same four features
drops the spread to $3$--$11\%$, so the noise floor has to be established per
experiment.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig2_interaction.png}
\caption{Same data, two views. OLS with all main and pairwise terms assigns
$a,b,c$ (and every pairwise product) essentially zero coefficient, seeing only
$d$; RF impurity importance ranks the three interacting variables highest. The
ordering among $a,b,c$ is sampling noise --- they are exchangeable by
construction.}
\label{fig:interaction}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Takeaway.} A high importance need not imply any marginal effect:
importance surfaces variables that matter only in combination, exactly the ones a
linear model drops. Importance and coefficients measure different things.

\section{What the split criterion sees, and what it discards}
\label{sec:variance}

Two properties of variance-based splitting explain the rest of the paper. Take a
noise-free, sorted dataset and sweep every single split, scoring each by the sum
of within-child variances, for $y = x$, $y = x^2$, $y = x^3$ ($x = 1\ldots1000$):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
response & raw min split-variance & frac.\ of parent variance removed & argmin index (of 1000) \\
\midrule
$x$   & $4.2 \times 10^{4}$  & $0.75$ & 500 (centre) \\
$x^2$ & $4.3 \times 10^{10}$ & $0.76$ & 697 \\
$x^3$ & $4.1 \times 10^{16}$ & $0.74$ & 799 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The raw minimum grows by orders of magnitude with the degree, but that is a
\emph{response-scale artifact}: as a fraction of parent variance removed, all
three splits are essentially equal (${\approx}0.75$). A ``large scale wins''
story does not survive normalisation; two things do.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item \textbf{Trees discard feature-distribution shape.} A split compares only the
  \emph{order} of a feature's values, so the tree is invariant to any monotone
  transform and cannot see whether a feature is uniform or heavy-tailed,
  information a per-unit effect size needs. This is the root cause of the confound
  in Section~\ref{sec:confound}.
\item \textbf{The optimal split migrates with response shape.} For a linear
  response the best split is central; for higher orders it moves toward the
  high-$y$ tail ($500 \rightarrow 697 \rightarrow 799$). The imbalance leaves more
  structure in the large child, so more splits follow down that branch, each
  charged to the same feature.
\end{enumerate}

So importance summarises \emph{variance contribution over the feature ordering},
blind to the metric the ordering lives on.

\section{Non-linear effects: variance contribution predicts the ranking}
\label{sec:nonlinear}

Data: $y = a + b^2 + c^3$, with $a = 1 + U_a$, $b = 1 + U_b$, $c = 1 + U_c$ and
$U_a, U_b, U_c$ independent $U(0,1)$ draws, $n = 10000$. (The draws are
\emph{separate} per feature: a single shared $U$ would make the three features
collinear and the experiment vacuous.)
Each term's variance contribution grows with its degree, so
Section~\ref{sec:background-strands} predicts
$\mathrm{vip}(a) < \mathrm{vip}(b) < \mathrm{vip}(c)$; the forest confirms it,
$1751 < 8166 < 39632$ (a $1:4.7:22.6$ ratio). A linear model still returns non-zero
coefficients, but they are uninterpretable: the residuals are visibly non-normal.

\section{Non-linearity and feature scale are indistinguishable}
\label{sec:confound}

The identity of Section~\ref{sec:background-strands} has a jarring consequence,
which the following construction makes concrete. Take equal linear effects but
skewed feature \emph{distributions}:
\[
  a = 1 + U_a, \quad b = (1 + U_b)^2, \quad c = (1 + U_c)^3, \quad y = a + b + c,
  \quad n = 10000,
\]
where $U_a, U_b, U_c$ are \emph{the same} draws used in
Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear}. Every feature enters with coefficient 1, and OLS
recovers $1.00, 1.00, 1.00$: nothing about the \emph{effects} is non-linear. Yet
the forest reports \textbf{1751 / 8166 / 39632}, a $1:4.7:22.6$ spread
\textbf{numerically identical}, to the digit, to the non-linear model of
Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear} (Fig.~\ref{fig:confound}).

That is not a coincidence of this seed but a theorem about the construction.

\paragraph{Proposition.} Fix draws $U_a, U_b, U_c$ and compare two worlds:
\emph{(A)} features $(1{+}U_a,\; 1{+}U_b,\; 1{+}U_c)$ with response
$y = a + b^2 + c^3$, and \emph{(B)} features
$(1{+}U_a,\; (1{+}U_b)^2,\; (1{+}U_c)^3)$ with response $y = a + b + c$. Then the
response vector is the same in both worlds, and each world-B feature is a
strictly increasing transform of its world-A counterpart. If (i) split selection
depends on a feature only through the \emph{ordering} of its values --- as it
does for an exhaustive search over thresholds between consecutive order
statistics, \code{ranger}'s default \code{splitrule = "variance"} --- and (ii)
bootstrap indices and candidate-feature draws are held fixed across the two fits,
then the two forests are identical tree for tree, and every importance derived
from them coincides exactly.

The mechanism is the monotone relabelling, not equality of variance
contributions. Equal variance is a by-product of the same construction; the
argument uses only that $y$ is the same vector and the features are in the same
order.

Both hypotheses can fail in practice. Hypothesis (ii) concerns the random number
generator: the two fits must draw the same bootstrap samples and candidate
features (and, for the debiased variant in the table below, the same
permutations). Reseeded, they agree exactly. Run one after the other in a single
session, as a naive script does, they differ by ${\sim}1\%$ --- comfortably
inside the ${\sim}2\%$ that refitting a single world under two seeds produces on
its own. Hypothesis (i) concerns the split search:
\code{splitrule = "extratrees"} draws thresholds uniformly from the feature's
value \emph{range} instead of scanning order statistics. Squaring stretches that
range, so the same draw lands elsewhere in the data and the two worlds separate
by ${\sim}3\%$ ($1:4.9:23.7$ against $1:5.0:24.6$).

Trees under (i) discard exactly the distribution-shape information that would
tell the two apart.

This is \textbf{not specific to impurity}, and debiasing does not touch it.
Bias-corrected impurity (AIR), permutation importance, and global mean
$|\text{SHAP}|$ all give the same ratios in both worlds:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
measure & ratio & cross-world agreement \\
\midrule
impurity (MDI)            & $1:4.7:22.6$   & exactly $0$ \\
impurity, debiased (AIR)  & $1:8.8:47.4$   & exactly $0$ \\
permutation               & $1:9.2:50.3$   & ${\sim}10^{-4}$ \\
mean $|\text{SHAP}|$      & $1:3.06:6.98$  & $1.3 \times 10^{-4}$ \\
\midrule
standardised OLS          & $1:2.98:6.88$ (A), $1:2.99:6.98$ (B) & $1.4 \times 10^{-2}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The first four are pinned by the proposition, in descending strictness. MDI is a
functional of the fitted trees alone, so it agrees identically. AIR agrees
identically for the same reason, one step removed: the correction subtracts the
impurity a permuted copy of each feature would earn, and under hypothesis (ii)
those permutation draws are fixed too, while permuting commutes with monotone
transforms --- the permuted copy of $(1+U_b)^2$ is the square of the permuted
copy of $1+U_b$ --- so the correction is a functional of the same trees and the
same orderings. Debiasing moves the ratio from plain MDI's compressed
$1:4.7:22.6$ toward the variance-contribution ratio $1:9.07:48.96$ (a single
draw; Section~\ref{sec:linear}'s caveat against reading any single-seed AIR
ratio closely applies here too), and moves the cross-world agreement not at
all. Permutation and
path-dependent TreeSHAP also touch feature values at the thresholds --- one
through the values it shuffles, the other through node weights it reconstructs
from the data, which differ by at most two observations on $0.3\%$ of nodes ---
and agree to four significant figures rather than exactly.

Standardised OLS is the control. It never sees the forest, so the proposition
gives it nothing, and the two worlds are genuinely different regressions:
projecting $y$ onto $1+U$ is not projecting it onto $(1+U)^2$. The gap between
the two has a closed form. With independent features, the standardised
coefficient of $b$ is $\mathrm{sd}(b^2)/\mathrm{sd}(y)$ in world B and
$\mathrm{Cov}(b^2, b)/(\mathrm{sd}(b)\,\mathrm{sd}(y)) =
\mathrm{Corr}(b, b^2)\,\mathrm{sd}(b^2)/\mathrm{sd}(y)$ in world A, so the two
worlds differ by exactly the factor $\mathrm{Corr}(b, b^2)$: the fraction of the
squared feature that a linear projection captures. For $U(1,2)$ these
correlations are $0.9963$ for the square and $0.9861$ for the cube, and the
table reproduces them: $2.98/2.99 = 0.997$ and $6.88/6.98 = 0.986$. The
discrepancy is the population correlation gap itself, two orders of magnitude
larger than anything the proposition-pinned measures show.

That spread of agreement is what identifies the mechanism. Equal variance
contribution alone would predict uniform approximate agreement, and cannot
produce an exact zero. Nor is being a variance the operative property: MDI and
permutation are denominated in variance, but mean $|\text{SHAP}|$ tracks the
\emph{square root} of the variance contribution ($\sqrt{1:9.07:48.96} =
1:3.01:7.00$), one power lower, and is confounded just the same. What the four
share is that each is a functional of the fitted model and the feature ordering,
which the proposition makes identical. Any marginal importance inherits this,
whatever its units. The remedy is a shape-aware view: a partial-dependence plot,
or a SHAP \emph{dependence} plot, which plots the same $\phi$ values against
feature values that do differ between the two worlds.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig3_confound.png}
\caption{Provably identical importances from two different worlds. Left: genuinely
non-linear effects. Right: linear effects on skewed features. No marginal
importance measure can distinguish them.}
\label{fig:confound}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Takeaway.} A tall importance bar can mean a strong or non-linear
effect, or merely a heavy-tailed feature; no marginal importance can say which.

\section{Practical guidance}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item \textbf{Read importance ordinally, not as an effect size.} Treat it as a
  ranked shortlist among comparable features, never as a magnitude to line up
  against OLS coefficients. A high bar may reflect a marginal effect, an
  interaction (Section~\ref{sec:interactions}), a non-linear effect
  (Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear}), or merely a skewed feature distribution
  (Section~\ref{sec:confound}).
\item \textbf{Disambiguate with partial-dependence plots.} PDPs~\cite{friedman2001}
  recover exactly what importance discards: in Section~\ref{sec:confound} the skewed-feature
  model's PDPs are straight lines while the non-linear model's curve --- same
  importances, different shapes. For the Section~\ref{sec:interactions}
  interaction, a one- or two-way PDP among $a,b,c$ is flat --- the parity pattern
  only appears in the full three-way view --- which is itself the tell that the
  effect is a higher-order interaction that per-feature importance has flattened.
  Curvature (or a suspiciously flat low-order PDP over a high-importance variable)
  is a concrete cue to add a polynomial or interaction term back into an
  interpretable model.

  \emph{With one caveat.} A PDP averages the model over the marginal
  distribution of the other features, which is exact here because our features
  are independent. When features are dependent it evaluates the model on
  combinations that do not occur in the data, and Hooker et
  al.~\cite{hooker2021} show that the resulting curve can report the model's
  extrapolation rather than any effect --- the same mechanism that inflates
  permutation importance for correlated predictors. On correlated data, use
  ALE~\cite{apley2020}, which accumulates local effects conditional on the
  neighbouring feature distribution instead of marginalising across it.
\item \textbf{\code{impurity\_corrected} fixes a different problem.} It removes
  the bias toward null / high-cardinality features (use it --- it is the right
  default), and it tracks the variance contribution more faithfully than plain
  impurity, which the default \code{mtry} compresses (Section~\ref{sec:linear}).
  What it does not change is the \emph{units}: it is still a variance, not a
  per-unit effect, so nothing in
  Sections~\ref{sec:interactions}--\ref{sec:confound} goes away --- the table
  in Section~\ref{sec:confound} shows it pinned across the two worlds exactly,
  like plain impurity. Debiased
  MDI variants exist~\cite{li2019,zhou2021} but target the same type-bias, not
  the shape/scale reading examined here.
\item \textbf{Switching to permutation importance does not fix the shape/skew
  conflation.} Permutation and conditional~\cite{strobl2008} importances address
  other problems (and are worth using), but as Section~\ref{sec:confound} shows
  they too summarise variance contribution and cannot separate effect shape from
  feature distribution. Only a shape-aware view (PDP/ALE~\cite{friedman2001,apley2020},
  SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017}) does that.
\item \textbf{If you know the structure is linear, use the linear model.} Random
  forests buy you interaction- and non-linearity-detection; when there is nothing
  to detect, OLS is both more accurate and directly interpretable.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Prevalence in the literature}

The preceding sections establish, in controlled settings, how a
variance-contribution measure can be misread as an effect size. To estimate how
often that misreading reaches published conclusions, we screened the literature
that uses the \code{ranger} R package --- one of the most widely used
random-forest implementations, and the one whose importances these results
concern. We screen for papers whose stated conclusions depend on the magnitude or
ranking of an impurity importance without the corroboration that would guard
against the effects of Sections~\ref{sec:linear}--\ref{sec:confound}.

\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:prevalence-method}

\paragraph{Corpus.} We took every work recorded by OpenAlex as citing the ranger
reference publication~\cite{wright2017} (OpenAlex work \code{W2157395790}),
$3{,}189$ works in total. For each we retrieved bibliographic metadata from
OpenAlex and, where an open-access full text was available, the full text from
Europe PMC; $773$ works had retrievable open-access full text. Screening was
restricted to these, because the detail needed to judge exposure --- which
importance measure is used, and whether it is corroborated --- is almost never
present in an abstract.

\paragraph{Relevance filter.} Many papers cite ranger only for its speed or for
prediction and never interpret a variable importance. To drop these without
discarding relevant work, we required a textual mention of variable importance: a
paper was retained if its full text matched, case-insensitively, any of the
following regular-expression alternatives.
\begin{verbatim}
(variable|feature|permutation|gini|impurity)\s+importance
importance\s+(score|value|ranking|measure)
mean decrease
decrease in (impurity|gini|accuracy)
\bMDI\b   \bMDA\b   \bBoruta\b
impurity[_\s]?corrected
most important (variable|feature|predictor)
\end{verbatim}
$524$ of the $773$ matched. The filter is deliberately broad: an earlier version
that also required a significance keyword (\code{importance\_pvalues}, Altmann,
Janitza, Boruta) missed most exposed papers. Of $40$ sampled papers it rejected,
$24$ still used an impurity importance and $11$ met the flag criterion below,
because many papers rank features by impurity importance with no significance
test. In total $544$ papers were screened: the $524$ matches plus $20$ from
earlier passes (including that probe) that the broadened filter misses. Its recall
was not separately re-estimated (see limitations).

\paragraph{Two-stage screen.} Each paper was read by a large language model under
a fixed instruction and asked to return a structured judgement; the verbatim
prompts are in the supporting repository (Section~\ref{sec:availability}). The
screen has two stages using different models, so that the second acts as an
adversarial check on the first.

\emph{Stage 1 --- screening.} A first model (Claude Haiku 4.5) read each paper and
recorded which importance measure it uses, whether it interprets that importance's
magnitude or ranking, which corroborating analyses it reports, and a flag for
whether a central conclusion could have been affected. The instruction was
explicit that the bias corrupts magnitude and ranking, not the binary verdict
that a variable is used, so a significant importance $p$-value
(Altmann~\cite{altmann2010}, Janitza~\cite{janitza2018}, Boruta~\cite{kursa2010})
does not by itself protect a conclusion. A paper was flagged only if it leaned on
the magnitude or ranking of an impurity importance for a central conclusion
\emph{without} corroboration, defined as permutation importance,
SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017}, partial-dependence/ALE~\cite{friedman2001,apley2020},
conditional importance~\cite{strobl2008}, or held-out validation of the
\emph{selected} features (overall test accuracy does not count, as it validates
prediction, not the ranking).\footnote{Counting SHAP as corroboration is
generous: Section~\ref{sec:confound} shows that a global mean $|\text{SHAP}|$
ranking carries the same shape/skew confound as impurity importance, while a
SHAP \emph{dependence} plot does not. The screen does not distinguish the two
forms, so papers reporting only a mean $|\text{SHAP}|$ ranking were credited
with corroboration they do not have. This can only suppress flags, so it makes
the confirmed count conservative.} Every flag required a quoted passage.

\emph{Stage 2 --- adjudication.} Every flagged paper was independently re-read by
a second, stronger model (Claude Sonnet 5) under an explicitly adversarial
instruction: its task was to \emph{refute} the flag. It returned a verdict of
\emph{confirmed}, \emph{refuted}, or \emph{uncertain}, and confirmed only when all
three conditions held at once: 1) the paper genuinely uses an impurity (not solely
a permutation or SHAP) importance, 2) the ranking is central to a stated conclusion,
and 3) the ranking is not adequately corroborated. Both models were accessed in
July 2026. Using two models from a single vendor means the adjudication is a
precision filter, not an independent validation --- a different-vendor or human
check would be stronger; we return to this in the limitations.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:prevalence-results}

Table~\ref{tab:prevalence} traces the corpus through the pipeline. Of the $544$
papers screened, $307$ were judged to use an impurity importance and $125$ were
flagged in stage~1. The adversarial adjudication confirmed $55$, refuted $65$, and
could not decide for $5$, overturning roughly $56\%$ of the stage-1 flags. The
implied stage-1 precision is therefore about $44\%$, and raw screening counts
should be discounted accordingly.

Taking the confirmed count in Table~\ref{tab:prevalence} at face value, $55$ of
the $544$ screened papers state a central conclusion that could have been
distorted by the effects of Sections~\ref{sec:linear}--\ref{sec:confound} and did
not cross-check it. That is about $10\%$ of the screened papers. Measured against
the $307$ that actually interpret an impurity importance, it is $18\%$, roughly
one in six. As a share of all $773$ full-text ranger-citing works it is about
$7\%$.

Two features of the confirmed set are worth drawing out. First, exposure is
concentrated in papers that use no significance machinery: $51$ of the $55$
confirmed papers ($93\%$) report no importance $p$-value or Boruta selection and
simply rank features by impurity importance, suggesting that familiarity with
significance testing goes with more cautious interpretation of a ranking. Second,
the confirmed papers are not confined to low-visibility venues: citation counts
span the full range of the corpus (median $11$, versus a corpus median of $10$),
and $12$ of the $55$ exceed $50$ citations.

\paragraph{Limitations.} These are model judgements, not audits: a flag means a
conclusion \emph{could} have been affected and was uncorroborated, not that it is
wrong, and predictive accuracy is unaffected. The $10\%$ is an unvalidated
estimate; stage 2 raises precision but is not independent validation, and there
is no human ground truth. Only $773$ of $3{,}189$ works had open-access full
text, and open access correlates with field and venue, so the sample may not
generalise. The broadened filter's recall was not re-estimated. Our controlled
experiments are all regressions, and the screen does not record which of the
screened papers are classification and which regression; the variance reading of
the Gini index in Section~\ref{sec:background-strands} is what carries the
caution across to the classification papers, and we did not verify it there
empirically. The repository
lists flagged papers by identifier; we treat this as a claim about corroboration
practice, not correctness, with each flag backed by a quoted passage.

\section{Conclusion}

Impurity importance estimates a feature's variance contribution, not its per-unit
effect. Read that way its behaviour is unsurprising: it scales with the square of
a linear coefficient, assigns an interaction's variance to the interacting
variables, and gives identical scores to a non-linear effect and to a linear
effect on a skewed feature. That last identity is provable, and it holds for
permutation importance too, so it is a property of variance-based measures rather
than a defect of MDI. The mechanisms are largely established; our contribution is
the sharp skew-versus-nonlinearity demonstration and an estimate of how far the
misreading reaches. In the screened literature, $18\%$ of the papers that
interpret an impurity ranking, including heavily-cited work, lean on it without
corroboration. Impurity importance is a useful screening tool when read as a
ranked shortlist and paired with partial-dependence plots. It misleads when read
as an effect size.
