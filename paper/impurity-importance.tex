\author{Christian Theil Have}

\begin{abstract}
Impurity-based variable importance (mean decrease in impurity) is the default,
cheapest way to read which features matter in a random forest regression, and it
is routinely misread as an effect size comparable to a linear-model coefficient.
What it actually estimates is each feature's contribution to the variance of the
response: for a linear effect, the square of the coefficient times the feature's
variance. Through controlled experiments we show that importance (i) grows with
the \emph{square} of a linear coefficient; (ii) assigns an interaction's
variance to the interacting variables, so a feature with no effect of its own
can top the ranking; and (iii) gives \emph{identical} scores to a genuinely
non-linear effect and to a linear effect on a skewed feature. These problems are
distinct from the long-known bias towards features with many distinct values.
Our closing point is that they cannot be escaped by switching measure:
bias-corrected impurity, permutation importance, and SHAP-based importance
scores all inherit them, because no importance measure that reduces a feature to
a single number can separate the \emph{shape} of its effect from the
\emph{spread} of its values.
\end{abstract}

\section{Controlled experiments}
The experiments that follow use synthetic data with known ground truth to isolate, one mechanism at a time, what impurity importance measures when it is read as an effect size. In each case the data-generating process is fully specified, the features are independent except where dependence is the point, and the impurity ranking is set beside the ordinary-least-squares coefficient on the same data --- the per-unit effect a reader is tempted to see in it. The unifying finding is that importance tracks each term's contribution to the variance of the response, $\mathrm{Var}(f_j(X_j))$, not its per-unit slope: it grows with the \emph{square} of a linear coefficient (Section~\ref{sec:linear}), credits an interaction's variance to the interacting variables even when neither has a marginal effect (Section~\ref{sec:interactions}), and therefore prices a non-linear effect at the variance it contributes (Sections~\ref{sec:variance}--\ref{sec:nonlinear}), leaving effect shape and feature spread exactly indistinguishable to any marginal importance measure (Section~\ref{sec:confound}).

material, in a single archive: the R scripts that generate the
controlled experiments and figures of
Sections~\ref{sec:linear}--\ref{sec:confound} (with a one-line command to
